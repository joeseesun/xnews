乔木改写

天琴座有一颗比较凉的恒星，大约只有太阳一半大，编号是KIC 5689121。OpenAI工程师Kevin Bond用Codex写的搜索程序发现，它的亮度大约每13.8天暗一次。如果这是一颗行星经过造成的，挡掉的光大约相当于地球直径的2.4倍。

这还不是已确认的行星。OpenAI在2026年11月24日的文章《Searching for distant planets with Astra》里写得很清楚：确认原因可能还要几个月到几年。和Bond无关的另一位搜寻者Ethan Byrd，则用Astra在另一批公开望远镜数据里做了独立搜索。

业余爱好者本来就是找行星的一部分

天文学家已经确认了太阳系外超过6400颗行星。这个数字来自NASA系外行星档案的Planetary Systems Composite Parameters表，2026年的部分统计到当年11月24日。第一批系外行星是1992年宣布的。

全世界的业余爱好者一直参与这件事。有人在公开望远镜数据里，找恒星亮度里那种又小又重复的下降。有人用自己的望远镜去守下一次变暗，再把看到的结果交给研究者。

用AI做这件事也不是从Astra才开始。2017年，研究者拿NASA开普勒空间望远镜里15,000个天文学家已经看过的信号，训练了一个神经网络。模型学会哪些星光变化更可能来自行星。他们再用它去看更弱的信号，其中一次把他们带到了Kepler-90这颗恒星的第八颗行星。

现在，像Astra这样的工具，在帮业余爱好者自己设计和执行搜索。对象常常是已经找出过更明显行星信号的那些数据。

Kevin Bond是OpenAI的工程师，入门天文学是因为喜欢拍夜空。他用Codex搜索了开普勒望远镜大约10万颗恒星的观测，这项行星搜寻任务的运行时间是2009年到2018年。Ethan Byrd是电子邮件基础设施创业公司Primitive的创始人，一直喜欢天文学。他先试了几款AI模型，最后选Astra来分析大约1900万颗恒星的望远镜数据。两人是各自做的。文章在介绍两位搜寻者时，放了三张拍摄夜空和在电脑前工作的照片。

两人都找到了可能的系外行星信号，并交给职业天文学家做独立评估。Bond联系了SETI研究院的一位天文学家，对方做了独立搜索，在开普勒数据里找到了同样的两个信号。Byrd把他最强的一个候选交给了NASA TESS任务的研究者，正在排队做进一步分析，还有两个候选等着提交。

他们找的是规律出现的亮度下降

常见做法是盯着行星绕转的那颗恒星。当行星从地球上看刚好从恒星前面经过，会挡住一点点星光。天文学家把这次经过叫做凌星。在亮度随时间变化的图上，也就是光变曲线上，凌星会表现为一次下降。如果下降按固定间隔反复出现，就知道下次该什么时候再看。

在成千上万颗恒星里找这种模式，有一部分是编程问题。天文学家已经发表过，怎样在多年测量里把微弱、重复的下降挑出来。Bond用Codex比较这些方法，把它们组合成搜索程序，并测试改代码会不会让搜索更好。一个AI智能体先在光变曲线里扫有希望的信号，另一个再对前者标出的候选做更细的检查。

Bond把程序跑在开普勒的公开数据上。搜索标出了200多个信号，其中10个来自此前已确认的行星。找到这些已知行星，让他更有信心：程序能认出他要找的那类信号。

两颗星比较突出。第一颗就是KIC 5689121，光变周期大约13.8天。文章配图把同一些开普勒观测，按13.77天的周期对齐，画成四条曲线。官网这一页是交互图，没有单独的静态图片地址。下面这张图来自OpenAI官方宣布帖，对应的是同一颗星的这段说明。

第二颗是KIC 6691030，比较暗，位于北天。搜索发现它大约每4.8天暗一次，如果是行星，大小比海王星小。SETI研究院那位天文学家独立找到的，就是这两个信号。

SETI研究院国际SETI项目副主管Vishal Gajjar的评价是：Kevin的工作说明，公民科学家在AI的帮助下，可以协助天文学家探索开普勒Bonus Catalog，也就是开普勒原先目标之外的一大批恒星。他的流程在传统搜索可能错过的数据里，找出了有希望的候选。初步检查令人鼓舞，后续观测会告诉我们更多。

另一边，Astra在大约1900万颗星里做了两轮搜索

Byrd让Astra设计了两种搜索。一种较宽，找一般的系外行星候选。另一种对准宜居带：恒星周围一段距离，如果行星有合适的大气，表面有可能存在液态水。

他接着让Astra查阅已发表的方法、编写搜索软件，并对有希望的信号做初步检查。数据来自NASA 2018年发射的TESS，以及其他公开望远镜档案。

开普勒四年里主要盯着一块天区。TESS在2018年到2020年的前26个观测区里，扫过南北两个半球。文章配图用的是NASA TESS镶嵌图，截止到2025年9月，并裁到这一轮巡天范围。图上较宽的空隙，是TESS在六个观测区里把指向挪得更北的地方。插图标出了KIC 5689121在原先开普勒视场中的位置。

第一轮数据跑下来，出现了49个候选。Byrd把其中最强的一个交给了TESS任务的研究者，正在等待进一步分析。他的搜索还在继续。

看到恒星变暗，还要排除别的解释

恒星变暗，不一定是行星。两颗恒星互相遮挡，也可能出现类似的下降。望远镜上温度一变，有时也够让仪器表现得像恒星变暗。

天文学家可以用统计说明，一连串下降最可能的解释是系外行星，也可以再用别的方法补充观测。比如，轨道上的行星会轻轻拉它的恒星，让恒星朝我们或背我们移动一点点。这种径向速度，会表现为星光颜色的微小偏移。随后还要把结果发到同行评审的期刊上，让别人检查证据。

对Bond和Byrd来说，每个候选都是一个可以检验的问题。如果天琴座那颗星真有行星每13.8天经过一次，天文学家可以去守下一次经过。后续观测可以看下降是否按预期重复，也可以用更多检查判断原因是什么。

文章在这里放了Kevin Bond拍摄的照片。

没有自己的望远镜，也可以参加

NASA的Exoplanet Watch可以申请数据，并学习自己画光变曲线。它还有更广的系外行星公民科学项目。有望远镜的人，可以按Exoplanet Watch的说明采集凌星系外行星的数据，并参加观测活动。AAVSO的系外行星观测项目可以教人测量星光下降，并把观测分享给研究这颗行星的人。Unistellar Network Investigating TESS Exoplanets则组织世界各地使用特定望远镜的志愿者，一起观测同一颗星，帮助抓住可能长过一夜的凌星。

作者的收束是：天文学里，本来就有世界各地的人跟着好奇心走，再分享自己的发现。Bond和Byrd也属于这个传统。他们的搜索还没有结束，但已经把对夜空的兴趣，变成了其他天文学家可以接着查的线索。

对想跟着做的人，可以先从Exoplanet Watch申请公开数据，学会画一条光变曲线，再决定要不要参加针对某个候选的后续观测。

Searching for distant planets with Astra

Searching for distant planets with Astra

Two amateur astronomers used Codex and Astra to look for signs of planets in public telescope data. Now they’ve given professional astronomers a few places to look more closely.

Astronomers have confirmed more than 6,400 planets beyond our solar system since the first ones were announced in 1992. A worldwide community of amateur astronomers has long been part of that search. Some comb through public telescope data for small, repeated dips in a star’s brightness that could signal an orbiting planet. Others use their own telescopes to watch for the next dip and share what they see with researchers.

*Data from the NASA Exoplanet Archive’s Planetary Systems Composite Parameters table. 2026 data are year to date through November 24, 2026.

How astronomers discover exoplanets

Astronomers have used AI in this search for years. In 2017, researchers trained a neural network on 15,000 signals from NASA’s Kepler Space Telescope that astronomers had already examined. From those examples, the model learned to recognize which patterns in starlight were most likely to come from a planet. When the researchers used it to examine weaker signals, one led them to an eighth planet orbiting a star called Kepler-90.

Now, tools like Astra are helping amateur astronomers design and run searches of their own, often on data that has already yielded the more obvious exoplanet signals. Kevin Bond, an engineer at OpenAI, came to astronomy through his love of photographing the night sky. He used Codex to search observations of about 100,000 stars from the Kepler telescope, a planet-hunting mission that ran from 2009 to 2018. Ethan Byrd, founder of the email infrastructure start-up Primitive, has always loved astronomy. Working independently of Bond, he tried several AI models before choosing Astra to help him analyze telescope data from about 19 million stars.

Both searches identified potential exoplanets. Bond and Byrd have now turned to astronomers for an independent assessment of their results. Bond contacted an astronomer at the SETI Institute, who ran an independent search and found two of the same signals in the Kepler data. Byrd sent one of his strongest candidates to researchers working on NASA’s Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) mission. It’s in line for further analysis, and two more candidates are awaiting submission.

Looking for dips in starlight

One of the most common ways to find an exoplanet is to watch the star it orbits. When a planet passes in front of its star from our point of view on Earth, it blocks a tiny amount of starlight. Astronomers call that crossing a transit. On a graph of the star’s brightness over time, known as a light curve, a transit shows up as a dip. If the dip keeps coming back at regular intervals, astronomers know when to watch for it again.

Searching for that pattern across thousands of stars is partly a coding problem. Astronomers have published methods for picking out faint, recurring dips in years of measurements. Bond used Codex to compare those methods, combine them in a search program, and test whether changes to the code improved the search. One AI agent scanned the light curves for promising signals. Another ran more detailed checks on the candidates the first one flagged.

Bond ran the program on public data from the Kepler Space Telescope. The search flagged more than 200 signals, including 10 from previously confirmed planets. Finding those known planets gave Bond confidence that the program could pick out the kinds of signals he was looking for.

Two stars stood out. The first, KIC 5689121, is a relatively cool star about half the size of our sun in the constellation Lyra. The Codex search found a dip in brightness every 13.8 days. If a planet is causing it, the amount of light it blocks suggests it could be about 2.4 times Earth’s diameter.

These four curves show the same Kepler observations aligned on a 13.77-day cycle.

The second star, KIC 6691030, is faint and lies in the northern sky. The Codex search found a dip in its brightness every 4.8 days, consistent with a planet smaller than Neptune. These were the two signals the SETI Institute astronomer found independently. Confirming what caused them could take months or years.

“Kevin’s work shows how citizen scientists, equipped with AI, can help astronomers explore the Kepler Bonus Catalog—a vast collection of stars beyond Kepler’s original targets. His pipeline has surfaced promising candidates in data traditional searches may have missed. Our initial checks are encouraging, and follow-up observations will tell us more.”

Vishal Gajjar, associate director for international SETI programs at the SETI Institute

Byrd used Astra to design two searches. One looked broadly for exoplanet candidates. The other focused on stars’ habitable zones. A habitable zone is the range of distances from a star where a planet with a suitable atmosphere could support liquid water on its surface.

Byrd then asked Astra to review published methods, build the search software, and run initial checks on promising signals. His searches drew on observations from TESS, which NASA launched in 2018, and from other public telescope archives.

Kepler watched one field for four years. TESS surveyed both hemispheres in its first 26 sectors, from 2018 to 2020. The star field comes from NASA’s TESS mosaic through September 2025, clipped to that original survey footprint. The wider gap shows where TESS pointed farther north during six observing sectors. The inset marks KIC 5689121 in the original Kepler field. See NASA’s final prime-mission coverage maps and its earlier field-of-view animation.

The first pass through the data turned up 49 candidates. Byrd sent one of his strongest candidates to researchers working on the TESS mission, where it is awaiting further analysis. His search is still running.

What does it take to confirm a planet?

When astronomers see a star dim, they have to consider what else might explain it. Two stars passing in front of one another can produce a similar dip. Even a change in temperature aboard a telescope can affect its instruments enough to make a star appear to dim.

Astronomers can use statistics to show that an exoplanet is the most likely explanation for a series of dips. They can also collect more observations, sometimes using a different method. For example, an orbiting planet’s gravity tugs on its star, causing it to move slightly toward and away from us. That motion, called radial velocity, shows up as tiny shifts in the color of the star’s light. Astronomers then publish their findings in a peer-reviewed journal so others can examine the evidence.

For Bond and Byrd, each candidate is a question that can be tested. If a planet is passing in front of the star in Lyra every 13.8 days, astronomers can watch for the next crossing. Further observations can help establish whether the dip repeats as expected, while additional checks can help determine what is causing it.

Taking part in the search

You can contribute without owning a telescope. NASA’s Exoplanet Watch lets you request data and learn to plot a light curve yourself. Its broader exoplanet citizen science programs offer other ways to participate.

If you have access to a telescope, Exoplanet Watch walks users through how to collect data on transiting exoplanets and participate in observation campaigns. AAVSO’s Exoplanet Observing Program can show you how to measure a dip in starlight and share your observations with the researchers studying that planet. And the Unistellar Network Investigating TESS Exoplanets coordinates volunteers around the world with a certain type of telescope to observe the same star, helping astronomers catch transits that can last longer than a single night.

Astronomy has a long history of people around the world following their curiosity and sharing what they find. Bond and Byrd are part of that tradition. Their searches are still open, but they turned a love of the night sky into leads that other astronomers can investigate. We want more people to have that opportunity, in astronomy and across science.
